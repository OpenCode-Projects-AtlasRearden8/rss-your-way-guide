# Chapter 3: Subscribing, Gently

Root of the word *syndication*, root of the method: you tell the reader where to look, and it shows up with what's new. This chapter covers the three big kinds of sources — blogs, podcasts, and library (and similar institution) newsletters — with a zero-guilt rule built into all of them.

## The golden rule first

**You do not owe anyone a subscribe.** You're not signing up to read everything they ever post. You're signing a contract that says: *if you post something, I'll see it,* and nothing more. You can drop anyone, quietly, later, without consequences and this is a feature, not a lapse.

## Three ways to find a source's feed, in order of effort

1. **Direct find.** Look for a button or link in the source's footer or sidebar that says **RSS**, **Atom**, **Feed**, **Subscribe**, or shows the RSS icon (a dot with two arcs, resembling a diamond of noise pointing away from you). Clicking it usually opens raw XML. Don't read it — copy the URL and paste it into your reader's "add subscription" box. The reader understands it.
2. **Reader's built-in search.** Many readers let you type "example.com" or a name and auto-discover the feed. Less manual, occasionally wrong; double-check it subscribed to the *right* feed.
3. **Guess-the-URL.** Common patterns worth trying: `example.com/feed/`, `example.com/rss`, `example.com/feeds/posts/default` (Blogger), `example.com/feed` (WordPress), `example.com/atom.xml`. Half the web lives on those.

Only as a last resort should you reach out to the site owner or a friend to ask. Hundreds of feeds exist that their own sites never advertise proudly enough.

## Blogs and written sites

Personal blogs and small publications are where RSS shines: one author, real voice, not graded by an algorithm.

- A WordPress site → try `/feed/`.
- A Substack → every Substack has a feed at `name.substack.com/feed`. Regular newsletters of that kind usually do too.
- A newspaper's Science desk, a university news page, a government agency's press page — many have per-section feeds. Subscribe to *the section*, not the whole paper, and the pile stays reasonable.
- Author blogs and small press sites: footer links, usually.

## Podcasts (you already half-do this)

Here's a secret that may reframe things: **every podcast app is a feed reader for audio.** The "subscriptions" in Apple Podcasts, Pocket Casts, AntennaPod, or Overcast are literally RSS feeds, and the free/subscribe line you see there is the same concept chapter 1 described.

So:

- **Keep podcasts in a podcast app; keep articles in your reader.** This is the healthy split, and it's fine that it's not all in one app.
- **Importing works the same:** find a show's RSS feed URL (many shows publish it right on their or their network's site; some apps let you share a raw feed URL) and paste it into your podcast app's "add by URL" spot.
- **The triage habit from chapter 5 applies to podcasts too:** short, well-labeled *saved queue* that resets, not an episode library growing like a landslide.

## Library and institution newsletters

Public libraries, museums, university continuing-ed offices, and local-history societies are criminally undersubscribed to, precisely because they announce things by email or a news page and it's easy to miss.

- **Ask your library's site for an RSS or "web feed"** — some have it (search the site for "RSS" or "feed"; some offer per-branch or per-events-category feeds).
- **No feed at all?** Sign up for the *email* newsletter anyway, then apply a filter (chapter 4) to route those emails into a folder that gets read on the same cycle as your feeds. The point of folder-and-filter organizing is that *this* is the technique for things that won't emit feeds. Same folder in chapter 4 can hold both.
- **Events feeds:** some libraries and museums publish an events page feed. Subscribing once means "what's happening at my library" is in the same list as your blogs. Local life, one inbox.

## A quick sanity exercise

Add *three* feeds this week, no more:

1. One blog you already read by typing its URL into your browser by hand.
2. One podcast you already listen to (import it by URL so you see the mechanics).
3. One institution — your library's events or news feed, or its email newsletter into your new folder.

Three is the goal. Notice what came in during the week, and whether reading it felt like a visit or a slog. Then decide who joins next, if anyone.
