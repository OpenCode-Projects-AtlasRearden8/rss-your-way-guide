# Chapter 4: Folders and Filters

Once you subscribe to more than a handful of sources, the reader's flat list of "everything new" starts to look messy. This chapter is about two tools — **folders** and **filters** — and a rule of thumb for keeping the pile approachable.

## Folders: the reading-mode groups

Most readers let you put subscriptions into folders (some call them categories). Don't organize by *topic taxonomy because it feels tidy*. Organize by **reading mode**, because that's how you'll actually consume them:

| Folder | Reading mode | Example contents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quick reads** | Two-minutes-and-done; casual | Personal blogs, friends' sites, comics |
| **Deep reads** | When there's time and focus to sit | Long essays, research blogs, a journal |
| **Local life** | Practical; act or note-and-move-on | Library events, neighborhood news, museum listings |
| **Tools and craft** | Skim; peek at only what's relevant this week | Tech blogs, tool release notes, industry feeds |

That's four. If a source fits none, make a fifth later; if it has to stretch to fit four, the folder list is lying to you and it'll be abandoned by winter. Folders exist to serve your sessions, not to look organized.

## Why folders earn their keep

- **Matching attention to feed.** On a rushed Tuesday, you open *Quick reads* and nothing else. On a Sunday with an hour, you open *Deep reads*. Local life gets checked when you're actually making plans.
- **Priority stays visual.** A folder you care about tells you the count. A folder you never open tells you it's time for the pruning exercise at the end of this chapter.
- **No doom potential.** There is no "all" view you have to scroll forever. The folders are finite on purpose.

## Filters: the inbox rules for the email side

Half your new sources won't be feeds at all — they'll be *email newsletters*, like the library's October calendar. That's fine; filters bring them into the same ecosystem.

In your email client (Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail, anything), make one filter:

- **If:** sender contains your library's (or museum's, or whatever's) newsletter address, or the word "newsletter" from that specific organization,
- **Then:** skip the inbox (**mark as read is optional — see below**), and apply a label/folder called **Newsletter reading** (or a name you'll actually check).

Two optional switches:

- **Skip inbox, keep unread.** Best for things you'd be sad to lose when the moment passes. Choose this for library announcements, event listings, and newsletters with real content.
- **Skip inbox, mark read.** For "nice to know, I glance at it" things. 

Whatever you choose: the point is that **your main inbox stays for people, and the reading pile gets its own room.** This immediately kills the guilt of "743 unread, one of them might matter."

## The escape hatch: OPML export

Here's the quiet power move. Every serious reader can **export your subscriptions as an OPML file** — a small XML document listing your feeds and folders. Find it in settings, usually under "Import/Export."

Once a month or so, click export. Save the file anywhere you back things up. It's not paranoia; it's the difference between "my reading list is *mine*" and "my reading list lives in an app." Same energy as keeping a paper notebook scan. If a reader ever goes away, ticks up a price, or changes shape, you import that OPML elsewhere and lose nothing.

## The once-a-season pruning exercise

A subscription list is a garden. About four times a year, when you happen to be in the reader anyway, take five minutes:

1. Open each folder, once.
2. Ask about each subscription: *Did I read this in the last two months, and did it ever give me something?*
3. If both answers are no, remove it. No ceremony. Unsubscribe in one click. It's a list, not a promise.

Subscribers lists are not friendships. Nobody is hurt. Nothing is owed. This is "windows I choose to leave open," and you may close them whenever.

## If you can't install a reader at all

Locked-down work computer? Library PC you can't install to? You still have comfortable options:

- Your **podcast app** follows this whole system for audio sources.
- A **news site's own newsletters**, routed via filters into folders, does 70% of the job.
- Some *web-based readers* run in a browser with no install (chapter 2's Feedbro is browser-native; FreshRSS is self-hosted web). If you can reach a website, you can use a reader.

Where there's a browser, there's a calm reading pile. The next chapter turns all of it into a small, kind, daily ritual.
